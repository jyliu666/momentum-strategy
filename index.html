Does stock momentum survive a clean backtest?

Eva Liu, MS Mathematical Finance, Boston University

LinkedInevaliu3639@gmail.com Prices to 28 October 2026; returns February 2015 to September 2026

Summary

I rebuilt a momentum stock-selection strategy from public data and tested it on the stocks that were actually in the S&P 500 on each date, with next-day execution, transaction costs, and a pass/fail rule written down before the first backtest ran.

  • The strategy failed its pre-registered test. Net of 15 bp costs it trailed an equal-weight portfolio of the same stocks by a year (Newey–West t = ) over 140 months. It returned 10.6% a year against 11.4% for equal weight and 14.0% for SPY.
  • Survivorship bias is large enough to manufacture a result. Run on today's index members, the same code returns 20.5% a year and beats SPY by 6.2 points (t = 1.93). About 10 points a year of that comes from using a list that could only be known in 2026.
  • The window decides the story. Over the last 36 months the strategy beat equal weight by 4.2% a year; over the full sample it did not. Momentum crashed in 2016 and in 2020–21.
  • A market-regime filter cut the maximum drawdown from −35% to −29% but lowered the Sharpe ratio, because it sold into rebounds.

The test, written down first

A backtest has many dials: factor weights, number of holdings, rebalance dates, start and end dates. Try enough combinations and one will look good. To rule that out, I fixed the strategy, the benchmark and the pass/fail rule in a dated file before computing any result. Everything else on this page is labelled as a secondary check.

Data: the index as it was

The most common mistake in an equity backtest is to take today's S&P 500 list and test it over the past decade. Companies are in today's list partly because they did well; the ones that were acquired, went bankrupt or were dropped are missing. Momentum buys recent winners, so it benefits more than most strategies from that error.

I rebuilt membership for every month-end from a public dataset that lists the full index on each change date since 1996 (fja05680/sp500 on GitHub), adding the six changes after August 2026 from the Wikipedia constituents table. Since January 2014, 789 tickers have been in the index. Prices for 618 Yahoo symbols were downloaded through the browser; 647 of the 789 tickers could be matched to a price history.

Coverage rises from about 76% of members in 2014 to 99% in 2026. The missing names are not random: they are mostly companies that were later acquired or failed. Some survivorship bias therefore remains in the early years, which is why every result is also reported from 2018 (coverage of at least 85%), and why the section on survivorship bias measures the size of the full error directly.

Signal: 22 price-based factors

All factors use daily open, high, low, close and volume up to the signal date. On each month-end every factor is converted into a robust z-score across the stocks in the index that day: (x − median) / (1.4826 × median absolute deviation), capped at ±3. A single stock that has tripled cannot then squash everyone else's score, as it would with a mean and standard deviation.

Momentum

Returns over 21, 63, 126 and 252 days; 12-1 momentum (12 months, skipping the latest month).

Risk and shape

21- and 63-day volatility, ATR %, downside deviation, overnight-gap volatility, 6-month max drawdown and skewness.

Trend and volume

Distance to 52-week high and low, price vs. 50- and 200-day averages, 50 vs. 200, RSI(14), dollar-volume z-score, volume trend, volatility breakout, volume spike.

The rank IC is the Spearman correlation between a factor's ranking and the next month's returns, computed each month. A mean IC of 0.03 to 0.05 is a useful single factor in practice. Figure 2 shows all 22 factors and the two blocks.

Results

Month-end close t: rank stocks→Close t+1: trade, pay 15 bp→From t+2: hold; weights drift with prices→Next month-end + 1: rebalance

SPY is capitalisation-weighted, and this was the decade of the mega-caps: every equal-weight portfolio trailed it, including the RSP ETF. Comparing the strategy with SPY therefore mostly measures equal weight against cap weight. The fair test of stock selection is the equal-weight portfolio of the same stocks.

The strategy did not pass. The net excess return is negative, the t-statistic is close to zero and the bootstrap interval runs from about −5% to +4% a year. Before costs the excess is a year, also far from significant. The 2018+ subsample gives the same answer. As agreed in advance, this is the result reported here and on my résumé.

How much survivorship bias is worth

To measure the error directly, I ran the identical code on a second universe: the S&P 500 members of October 2026, applied to every month back to 2015. Today's list contains later winners that joined the index only after their run (Tesla in 2020, Palantir in 2024, AppLovin in 2025) and none of the companies that were taken over or dropped.

With today's list the strategy returns 20.5% a year and beats SPY by 6.2 points with t = 1.93, close to the usual threshold for significance. Even the equal-weight portfolio, which selects nothing, gains about 5 points a year. All of it comes from knowing which companies would still be in the index in 2026.

Three years vs. eleven

Momentum earns steady small gains and gives them back in short, violent reversals. When a falling market turns, the stocks that fell most rebound first, and a momentum portfolio does not own them. That happened in 2016 (energy and materials) and in 2020–21 (the post-COVID rebound).

Costs and turnover

The portfolio replaces about 36% of its holdings each month, roughly 4.3 times a year one-way. At 15 bp per side that costs about 1.4 percentage points a year, which is more than the gross excess return. Move the slider to see how the conclusion depends on the cost assumption.

15 bp

Each month, net return = gross return − (cost ÷ 15 bp) × that month's cost at 15 bp; this matches the full backtest exactly at 0 and 15 bp.

Risk and the market-regime filter

The secondary variant scales exposure by market regime, defined in advance from SPY at the signal date: bear if SPY is below 97% of its 200-day average, or down more than 8% over 63 days and 4% over 21 days (50% invested); bull if SPY is above its 200-day average with a positive 63-day return (100%); otherwise neutral (75%). Cash earns the T-bill rate.

The filter lowered volatility and cut the worst drawdown from −35% to −29%, but it also lowered the return to 8.7% and the Sharpe ratio to 0.44. In the 14 bear-signal months SPY rose 3.5% on average the following month: sharp falls are often followed by sharp rebounds, and a half-invested portfolio misses them. As a risk-reduction tool the filter works; as a source of return it does not.

Robustness

None of the planned variants changes the conclusion. The machine-learning version uses gradient-boosted trees on all 22 factors, retrained every month on data whose labels were already known at the prediction date (expanding window, first prediction after 36 months, so it starts in 2018). Conclusion

On the stocks that were actually in the S&P 500, with next-day execution and realistic costs, this momentum strategy did not beat an equal-weight portfolio of the same stocks from 2015 to 2026. Over the most recent three years it looks strong, and on today's index list it looks excellent. Both views come from choices about the data and the window rather than from the signal.

The useful output of the project is the procedure: a point-in-time universe, careful treatment of renamed and reused tickers and bad adjustment factors, a fixed test, the right benchmark, significance tests that allow for autocorrelation, and a direct measurement of the biases. That procedure turned a result that looked strong into one that I can defend.

Limitations and methods

  • Coverage. Yahoo has no prices for delisted companies, so 24% of members are missing in 2014, about 14% in 2018 and 1% in 2026. Results from 2018 are reported separately. The "today's members" comparison measures survivorship bias and look-ahead in stock selection together.
  • Benchmark. Without market-cap data the main benchmark is equal-weighted, which matches the strategy's breadth but not SPY's weights.
  • Costs. A flat cost per side, with no market impact or borrowing; the strategy is long-only.
  • Scope. One market, monthly rebalancing, daily price data only; no fundamentals.
  • Statistics. Newey–West t-statistics with 3 lags; moving-block bootstrap with 6-month blocks; rank (Spearman) IC.
  • Independent check. A separate reviewer rebuilt the backtest from the raw files with new code and reproduced every headline number to within 0.0001%. The review also verified each ticker mapping against company filings and S&P announcements; the four data problems it found are fixed above and moved the main result by about 0.01 percentage points.
  • Tools. Python (pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, matplotlib). Three scripts rebuild every number from the raw files: data preparation, factors, backtest. Code available on request.

This is an independent project using public data and standard, published methods (cross-sectional momentum, volatility and trend factors, regime filters).
